# Research: what a good carrier and driver app looks like

Five parallel research passes run 2026-09-20 for the naviG8r roadmap. The short, ranked version
that feeds the roadmap is [`RECOMMENDATIONS.md`](RECOMMENDATIONS.md); this is the evidence behind it.

**Provenance labels.** `measured` is a study or a published number. `documented` is a vendor or a
product describing its own behaviour — self-description, not evidence that it works. `observed` is
a review, teardown or journalist account.

Every row carries a source. Where a pass found nothing, it says so under Gaps. Nothing here was
independently re-verified by me; it is research output with its sources attached.

## Rideshare driver apps — Uber and Lyft

| Finding | Evidence | Implication for naviG8r | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uber's driver offer card shows an upfront payout, the destination (as cross streets nearest pickup and dropoff), and estimated time and mileage. The upfront figure covers base fare, estimated trip length and duration, pickup distance and surge, but explicitly excludes tips, tolls, other surcharges and wait-time payments, which are added after the trip. | `documented` | A freight offer card needs the same four items plus the ones Uber does not have to carry: tonnage, commodity, vehicle type, loading/unloading responsibility and detention terms. The lesson to copy is that the headline number must be named as what it is: 'freight charge, before detention and demurrage', not 'you will earn X'. | [help.uber.com](https://help.uber.com/en/driving-and-delivering/article/upfront-fares?nodeId=bc83ed7e-6725-41de-afcb-72d263e5589f) |
| Uber commits in writing that where pickup and dropoff addresses are unchanged, the upfront fare can only increase; it recalculates upward on unexpected traffic or an address change, and the driver keeps the full upfront fare if they finish faster than estimated. | `documented` | This is the single most copyable commitment in the lens: a one-way ratchet on the quoted number. For naviG8r, 'the quoted freight for this lane can go up if the load changes, never down' is a promise that costs little, is trivially auditable, and directly answers the owner-operator's main fear about app-set rates. | [help.uber.com](https://help.uber.com/en/driving-and-delivering/article/upfront-fares?nodeId=bc83ed7e-6725-41de-afcb-72d263e5589f) |
| Lyft shows earnings, pickup and dropoff, estimated time and distance and a map before acceptance, and added an estimated dollars-per-hour rate on the accept screen so the driver does not have to do the arithmetic. Upfront pay excludes tips, tolls and bonuses, and is not offered at all in Washington State, New York City or Portland. | `documented` | The derived per-hour (or for freight, per-day and per-km-net-of-fuel) figure is the part worth stealing. An Indian owner-operator is comparing a lane against diesel, tolls and days away from home; showing the raw freight amount alone makes them do that maths on a phone at a dhaba. | [help.lyft.com](https://help.lyft.com/hc/en-us/all/articles/8668928544) |
| Lyft pays above the upfront amount when a ride runs five or more minutes longer than the original estimate, and does not claw back when it runs short. | `documented` | A published, automatic overrun threshold is the honest version of detention pay. naviG8r could define one concretely (for example, waiting beyond N hours at loading auto-adds detention at a published rate) rather than leaving detention to a dispute after the fact, which is where Indian road freight normally loses drivers money. | [help.lyft.com](https://help.lyft.com/hc/en-us/all/articles/8668928544) |
| In Australia and New Zealand, Uber gates the estimated trip duration and direction behind Uber Pro Gold, Platinum or Diamond status, and those tiers require keeping an acceptance rate of 85 percent or above. | `documented` | Direct anti-pattern. naviG8r should never make information about a load conditional on how often the driver says yes. The information a driver needs to judge a load is not a reward. | [www.uber.com](https://www.uber.com/nz/en/blog/pro-anz-duration-direction) |
| Uber's acceptance rate is computed over a rolling lookback of the 100 most recent exclusive requests, and letting a request time out counts against it exactly as an active decline does. Uber states acceptance rate affects Pro tier eligibility and eligibility for promotions or incentives. | `documented` | If naviG8r ever scores acceptance, a timeout must not equal a refusal. A truck driver may be loading, eating, or out of signal. Penalising silence converts a connectivity problem into a reputation problem, which is a bug the driver cannot fix. | [www.uber.com](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/understanding-acceptance-and-cancellation-rates/) |
| A working paper using roughly 2 million trips from 647 US Uber drivers over 2021-2025 (data from the Workers' Algorithm Observatory) estimates that Uber's 2022 move to algorithmic wage setting cut drivers' average base pay by about $1 per trip, or 6.3 percent of average trip earnings, controlling for worker and trip characteristics. The decline is larger for drivers who reject fewer offers: a 10 percentage point lower rejection rate implies a further 11 cents per trip, about $14 per month. | `measured` | Load-bearing caveat: the PDF is headed 'Preliminary: Comments welcome. Please do not cite or circulate', dated April 2026. Single study, not replicated. Treat the direction as a real risk, not the coefficient as a fact. The design implication for naviG8r is a hard rule: never let a driver's past willingness to accept low rates feed the rate offered to them. That is the mechanism this paper claims to detect. | [economics.ucr.edu](https://economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AlgorithmicWageDisc_JyotsanaKala.pdf) |
| The same dataset shows average pass-through from passenger fare to driver base pay of 54 percent, and that Uber's take rate rises as the per-minute fare rises, so the driver's share falls on the highest-value trips. The author's conclusion is that drivers are relatively better off taking several low-fare trips than a single high-fare trip. | `measured` | If naviG8r's commission is a flat percentage, say so and publish it. A commission that silently scales with lane value is the exact pattern being litigated and legislated against, and a carrier with a spreadsheet will detect it faster than a rideshare driver did. | [economics.ucr.edu](https://economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AlgorithmicWageDisc_JyotsanaKala.pdf) |
| A CSCW study analysed 1,392,776 comments and 65,377 posts from r/uberdrivers and r/lyftdrivers (2019-2022) with an LLM pipeline, plus nine driver interviews annotating app screenshots. Promotions drew the most concerns (11,490), then fares (9,083), then task allocation (7,221), then routes (3,546). The single largest individual category was 'drivers unsure about opaque fare calculation methods' at 23 percent (7,202 concerns); 'issues understanding and qualifying for incentive programs' was 21 percent (6,679). | `measured` | The ranking is the finding. Drivers complain about not understanding the incentive scheme more than about the base rate itself. If naviG8r adds bonuses, referral credits or lane incentives, the explanation of how to qualify is more important than the size of the bonus. An unexplained incentive generates more anger than no incentive. | [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10768) |
| In the same study, the design interventions drivers themselves asked for were: clearer promotion explanations, an upfront fare breakdown, a hold or pause button so a trip offer can be reviewed safely rather than under time pressure, and post-hoc explanations of why the algorithm assigned a given job. | `measured` | The hold/pause button is the cheapest of these and maps straight onto a truck driver who is moving when an offer lands. 'Why did I see this load?' is also answerable cheaply in naviG8r's current design, because matching is on declared lanes, so the explanation is a sentence, not a model interpretation. | [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10768) |
| Consumer Reports tested Uber and Lyft pricing in March and April 2026 using 174 volunteers across more than 40 routes, plus 12 simultaneous in-person tests in Portland. On the 30 virtual routes the median difference between the highest and lowest price groups was 42.4 percent for the identical trip. It also found the two platforms take between 43 and 49.5 percent of each fare, and that 12.4 percent of advertised discounts were fictitious, meaning an inflated reference price. | `measured` | Two anti-patterns named by an independent tester with a real method. For naviG8r the mirror risk is quoting different freight rates to identical shippers on the same lane, or showing a 'discounted' rate against a reference nobody ever paid. Both are detectable and both are now the subject of published journalism. | [www.consumerreports.org](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/uber-lyft-different-prices-for-same-ride-and-fake-discounts-a1093538909/) |
| FairFare, built with a Colorado rideshare union, crowdsourced driver data covering 76,000+ trips from 45 drivers over 18 months (later reported as 800+ drivers and 800,000+ rides) to estimate the platform take rate, because the platforms do not disclose it. Organisers reported it influenced the language and passage of Colorado SB 24-075. | `measured` | If naviG8r does not publish its own cut, someone will reconstruct it, and the reconstruction will be less flattering than the truth. Publishing the per-trip take rate as a line item is cheap insurance and a differentiator against incumbent Indian brokers, whose commission is invisible by design. | [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11273) |
| Colorado SB 24-075, effective 1 February 2025, requires TNCs to show drivers and riders on a single screen the total the rider paid before tip, the amount that went to the driver, and the tip. Uber sued in federal court to block it. | `observed` | 'On a single screen' is the operative phrase and is the design requirement, not the disclosure itself. Splitting the shipper's payment and the driver's payout across two screens defeats the purpose. naviG8r should build the single-screen settlement view now, while it is one component, rather than retrofit it later. | [towardsjustice.org](https://towardsjustice.org/2024/06/07/press-release-rideshare-industry-transparency-bill-becomes-law/) |
| A National Employment Law Project fact sheet (July 2025) collates published figures: Columbia Business School research putting Uber's take rate at 32 percent at the start of upfront pricing rising to 42 percent by end-2024; average weekly Uber driver earnings falling from $531 (2023) to $513 (2024) and Lyft from $370 to $318; and an Oxford study finding UK hourly driver pay fell from GBP 22.20 to GBP 19.06 before expenses. It also records that Uber stopped publishing its global rideshare take rate with quarterly shareholder reports as of Q1 2025. | `observed` | NELP is an advocacy organisation, so treat the framing as argument and the underlying citations as the evidence. The durable lesson is the last item: withdrawing a metric you previously published is read as an admission. naviG8r should only start publishing numbers it is prepared to keep publishing. | [www.nelp.org](https://www.nelp.org/app/uploads/2025/07/Unpacking-Uber-Lyfts-Take-Rates-July-2025-Update.pdf) |
| EU Directive 2024/2831 on platform work entered into force 1 December 2024 with transposition due by 2 December 2026. Article 6 requires platforms to inform workers about automated monitoring and automated decision-making systems that significantly affect working conditions, in a document also available to worker representatives and authorities; the directive requires human oversight of significant decisions and restricts certain automated decisions such as account suspension. | `documented` | India has no equivalent in force, so this is a roadmap signal rather than a compliance requirement. The cheap move is to design deactivation and load-matching with a human-review path and a written explanation from the start, because retrofitting human oversight into an automated suspension flow is the expensive version. | [en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_Work_Directive_2024) |
| Uber's driver app has an 'Optimistic Mode' that lets a driver end a trip with no connection and retrieve the last price estimate received from the server. Pending requests and optimistic state are serialised to disk so they survive app restarts; dependent requests (such as ending a trip the backend has not yet registered as started) are queued behind their parent; server errors roll state back, and Uber added a global Alert Framework because silent rollbacks left drivers in unexpected states. Uber reported an average 13.5 seconds saved per optimistic operation as of November 2018. | `documented` | This is the strongest single import for naviG8r. Accept load, start trip, confirm delivery and GPS breadcrumbs should all be queued to disk and replayed, not blocked on network. The rollback lesson is equally important: when a queued action is rejected by the server, the driver must be told plainly, not silently returned to a previous state. On a Nagpur-Raipur lane, a silent rollback of 'delivery confirmed' is a payment dispute. | [www.uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-IN/blog/driver-app-optimistic-mode/) |
| Uber Lite was built to under 5MB download and under 25MB on device, targets sub-300ms screen-to-screen response, pushes computation to a thin server-side orchestration layer to keep network payloads under one MTU, makes the map optional because maps consume bandwidth, and caches static data and search locations for offline use. Uber's stated motivation: 56 percent of global Uber riders used Android phones from Year Class 2015 or earlier, 33 percent of rides occur over sub-3G data networks, and 46 million Uber riders in India use Android phones equivalent to 2013 technology or older. | `documented` | These are Uber's own measurements of its own users, so treat the numbers as self-reported, but the India figures are the closest published proxy for naviG8r's driver population. The design rules transfer directly: make the map optional rather than mandatory, keep the APK small, and never make a required driver action depend on rendering a map tile. | [www.uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-ES/blog/engineering-uber-lite) |
| Uber's 'Verify My Ride' gives the rider a 4-digit PIN; the driver must enter it in the driver app to start the trip, with five attempts allowed. | `documented` | A short numeric code entered by the driver, supplied by the counterparty, is the lowest-friction proof mechanism in the lens and works offline if validated against a code issued at dispatch. For freight this maps onto loading confirmation at the shipper's gate and delivery confirmation at the consignee, where the consignee's storekeeper reads out a code rather than the app needing signal. Caveat: I verified the mechanic, not whether it reduces disputes. | [help.uber.com](https://help.uber.com/en/riders/article/whats-virtual-ride/?nodeId=2ddbb5e8-0dd3-4048-b9ee-f6b5e5311e25) |
| Uber Eats always captures a photo as proof of delivery for non-contact deliveries, downloadable from the merchant dashboard, and prompts the courier for the relevant proof of delivery before the delivery can be completed for non-food items. Rideshare trips themselves have no equivalent proof step; they end when the driver taps to end the trip. | `documented` | The blocking prompt is the pattern: the completion action is not available until the proof is captured. For naviG8r's electronic proof of delivery, gating 'confirm delivery' on a photo plus code is a one-screen change that removes the single largest category of freight payment dispute. The photo must queue offline like everything else. | [help.uber.com](https://help.uber.com/en/merchants-and-restaurants/article/delivery-faqs?nodeId=39d229f3-4375-4cd8-848b-9df7d19102c1) |
| Uber pays a Long Pickup Premium at standard per-mile and per-minute rates once a city-specific threshold is passed on the way to pickup, capped at $20; drivers are otherwise unpaid for miles driven to reach a passenger. | `documented` | The freight analogue is the empty run to the loading point, which in Indian road freight is routinely uncompensated and is the main reason an owner-operator refuses an otherwise good lane. Showing the empty-run distance on the offer card, and pricing it explicitly, is a concrete differentiator naviG8r can build on lane data it already has. | [help.uber.com](https://help.uber.com/en/riders/article/long-pickup-premiums?nodeId=f3d14b75-7d9a-4938-a6d4-d0b2033ad13f) |
| Uber charges $1.75 per Instant Pay cash-out (free in NYC), up to 6 times a day, and otherwise sweeps earnings to the bank on Mondays at 4am local. Lyft charges $1.75 per Express Pay transfer with a $6.75 minimum, up to 5 times per 24 hours, and otherwise pays weekly on Tuesdays. | `documented` | Both platforms charge the driver to reach money already earned. For naviG8r this is a live design fork in the payouts feature: a fee for fast settlement is the incumbent behaviour and is defensible only if the underlying rail actually costs money. In India, UPI and IMPS settlement costs are low enough that a per-cash-out fee would be hard to justify as cost recovery rather than a toll. | [help.lyft.com](https://help.lyft.com/hc/en-us/all/articles/115012923167-Express-Pay) |
| A May 2025 survey of 419 US gig drivers found 70 percent prefer daily or instant pay, 85 percent say fast access to earnings is 'very' or 'extremely' important when choosing a platform, 59 percent would leave a platform over sudden pay-rate or bonus drops, 44 percent would quit if instant payouts became slower or more expensive, and 65 percent had taken payday loans because of payout delays. | `measured` | Material conflict of interest: the survey was published by Everee, a vendor selling instant-pay infrastructure, so the questions favour its product and the numbers should not be treated as independent. Even discounted, the direction matches what is known about cash-flow pressure on Indian owner-operators, where the standard alternative is a broker paying 30 to 60 days out. Fast, predictable settlement is plausibly naviG8r's strongest wedge, and is worth validating with its own drivers rather than on this survey. | [www.everee.com](https://www.everee.com/blog/gig-report/) |
| Uber's Trip Radar shows a list of nearby rides that all eligible drivers see simultaneously, with the first to accept winning the job. | `observed` | A broadcast race rewards reaction time over fit, and is dangerous when the person racing is driving a loaded truck. If naviG8r broadcasts loads to multiple drivers, it should hold the offer open for a stated window and resolve ties on suitability, not on who tapped first. | [www.ridesharingdriver.com](https://www.ridesharingdriver.com/uber-driver-upfront-fares/) |
| More Perfect Union ran seven Uber and Lyft drivers on the same Los Angeles route at the same time and reported a pay discrepancy 63 percent of the time, with one or more drivers offered less for exactly the same ride. Uber rejects the claim that fares are personalised and states that a driver's race, ethnicity, acceptance rate, total earnings or prior trip history are not used in calculating fares. | `observed` | Very small sample and a campaigning organisation, so this is a claim in dispute, not a finding. Its value to naviG8r is as a reputational scenario: if two drivers on the same lane ever compare offers and find different numbers, naviG8r needs a published reason on the offer card itself. A rate that cannot be explained in one line will be read as discrimination whether or not it is. | [www.npr.org](https://www.npr.org/2023/04/25/1171800324/rideshare-drivers-raise-questions-about-how-algorithms-set-drivers-pay-rates) |

**Worth copying**

- One-way ratchet on the quoted number. Publish that the quoted freight can rise if the load changes but can never fall below what was shown at acceptance. Uber makes exactly this commitment for unchanged pickup and dropoff, and it is the single cheapest trust-builder in the lens.
- Derived rate on the offer card. Lyft added estimated dollars per hour so drivers stop doing mental arithmetic. naviG8r's version: net per kilometre after estimated diesel and tolls, and days away from home, computed by the app rather than by the driver at a roadside stop.
- Published overrun threshold. Lyft pays more when a ride exceeds its estimate by five or more minutes and never claws back when it is faster. The freight version is automatic detention pay after a stated number of hours at loading or unloading, triggered by the app rather than by a dispute.
- Empty-run priced on the offer card. Uber's Long Pickup Premium pays standard rates past a threshold. naviG8r should show the distance to the loading point and price it, because the uncompensated empty run is the main reason an owner-operator refuses a lane in Indian road freight.
- Optimistic mode with disk-persisted queues. Accept, start trip, breadcrumb and confirm delivery should all complete locally and replay when signal returns, surviving app restarts, with dependent actions queued behind their parents. This is Uber's documented architecture and it is a direct answer to naviG8r's current 'GPS only while one screen is open' limitation.
- Loud rollback, never silent. Uber built a global Alert Framework specifically because silently reverting an optimistic action left drivers in states they could not explain. Any queued naviG8r action the server later rejects must produce a named, plain-language message.
- Blocking proof gate. Uber Eats makes the completion action unavailable until proof of delivery is captured. Gate 'confirm delivery' on photo plus consignee code, queued offline, and the largest category of freight payment dispute mostly disappears.
- Counterparty code, not signature. A short numeric code read out by the shipper or consignee and typed by the driver validates against a code issued at dispatch, so it works with no signal and no signature pad.
- Single-screen settlement. Colorado requires the total the customer paid, the amount to the driver and the tip on one screen. Build naviG8r's settlement view that way from the start: the shipper's freight, naviG8r's cut, detention, deductions and the net, together.
- Map optional, not mandatory. Uber Lite makes the map an opt-in because tiles are the heaviest payload. No required driver action in naviG8r should depend on a map rendering.
- Small binary, thin client. Uber Lite targets under 5MB download, under 25MB installed, sub-300ms screen transitions and payloads under one MTU, with computation pushed server-side. These are concrete acceptance thresholds naviG8r can adopt as build-time budgets.
- Hold-and-review on an offer. Drivers in the CSCW study asked for a pause button so an offer can be read safely rather than under a countdown. For a driver in a moving truck this is a safety feature, not a convenience.
- Explain the assignment. Drivers asked for post-hoc explanations of why a job was routed to them. naviG8r matches on declared lanes, so the explanation is one sentence and costs almost nothing to render.
- Explain the incentive before the incentive. The single largest complaint cluster in the CSCW data was not understanding promotions. Any naviG8r bonus needs its qualification rule stated in the same view as the bonus amount.
- Publish the take rate per job. FairFare showed that an undisclosed cut gets reconstructed by outsiders and published less favourably than the truth. A visible commission line is a differentiator against Indian brokers whose margin is invisible by design.

**Do not copy**

- Gating job information behind acceptance rate. Uber's ANZ Pro programme requires an 85 percent acceptance rate before a driver may see trip duration and direction. Information needed to judge a job must never be a loyalty reward.
- Counting a timeout as a refusal. Uber's acceptance rate treats an expired request identically to an active decline over a 100-request lookback. For a driver who is loading, eating or out of coverage, this converts a connectivity problem into a permanent reputation problem.
- Decoupling what the customer pays from what the worker earns, then not disclosing the gap. This is the core of the take-rate controversy, with Columbia research cited at 32 percent rising to 42 percent and Consumer Reports measuring 43 to 49.5 percent of fare retained.
- Inferring pay from a worker's willingness to accept low offers. The UCI working paper finds the pay cut is larger for drivers who reject less, consistent with the algorithm learning reservation wages from engagement history. Preliminary and unreplicated, but it is the exact mechanism naviG8r must rule out by design.
- Charging a fee to reach money already earned. Uber's $1.75 Instant Pay and Lyft's $1.75 Express Pay both toll early access to earned income, against defaults of weekly Monday or Tuesday settlement.
- Fictitious reference prices. Consumer Reports found 12.4 percent of advertised discounts on the two platforms were inflated-original-price discounts.
- Different prices for the identical job with no stated reason. Consumer Reports measured a 42.4 percent median gap between highest and lowest price groups on identical virtual routes.
- First-to-accept broadcast racing. Trip Radar rewards reaction speed over suitability, which is actively unsafe when the person racing is driving.
- Withdrawing a metric you previously published. Uber stopped publishing its global rideshare take rate with quarterly shareholder reports as of Q1 2025, which reads as an admission regardless of the reason.
- Silent rollback of an offline action. Uber had to build an alert framework precisely because reverting optimistic state without telling the driver was worse than the original failure.
- Showing an estimate that can fall after acceptance. Uber's floor commitment only holds while pickup and dropoff are unchanged; without an explicit stated floor, an 'estimate' is a number the platform can walk back.

**Gaps — looked for, not found**

- No controlled or quasi-experimental evidence that showing more information before acceptance actually improves driver earnings, retention or satisfaction. Every source in this pass argues from complaint volume, take-rate accounting or advocacy, not from a trial. The case for pre-acceptance transparency is strong on ethics and regulation and unmeasured on outcomes.
- No primary-source confirmation of how long a driver has to accept an Uber request. A secondary blog reports the prior window was 10 seconds and has since been lengthened, and Uber's own acceptance-rate page does not state a duration. Treat the countdown length as unverified.
- Could not verify in the text of Colorado SB 24-075 itself that fare, distance and direction must be disclosed BEFORE acceptance. Secondary coverage asserts it; the bill summary page I fetched describes only 'specified disclosures' without timing, and I did not read the enrolled bill text. The post-trip single-screen requirement is well corroborated; the pre-acceptance clause is not, by me.
- No evidence found that Washington HB 2076 imposes any pre-acceptance disclosure requirement. The law covers minimum per-mile and per-minute rates, paid sick leave, workers' compensation and deactivation appeals. The Drivers Union FAQ I fetched contains no disclosure provisions.
- Nothing found on rideshare driver apps doing multilingual beyond static UI locale. Uber's driver app supports Hindi, Bengali, Kannada, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu, but this is device-locale string translation. I found no evidence of any rideshare platform accepting free-text or voice driver input in an Indian language and interpreting it, and therefore no precedent to copy for the LLM-interpreted multilingual feedback idea. That part of the roadmap has no proven analogue in this lens.
- No rideshare analogue to a freight electronic proof of delivery. Rides end on a driver tap with no artefact; the PIN is a start-of-trip identity check, and photo proof exists only on the delivery side of the business. Any naviG8r proof-of-delivery design is borrowing from delivery, not from rideshare.
- No India-specific research on what Indian rideshare drivers complain about. All the driver-side complaint research located here is US or UK (Reddit corpora, US interviews, Oxford UK pay study). The complaint ranking may not transfer to Indian owner-operators, and should be checked against naviG8r's own Slack feedback before being treated as a requirement.
- No independent verification that Lyft's 70 percent weekly earnings commitment actually pays out as described. Lyft documents the mechanic; NELP argues the deduction of unspecified costs and fees before the 70 percent calculation makes it far weaker than it sounds. I found no neutral audit of either claim.
- No measured evidence on how often optimistic-mode rollbacks confuse or harm drivers. Uber states it built the Alert Framework in response to the problem, which is documentation that the problem existed, not a measurement of its size.
- No data on Uber or Lyft driver-app crash, ANR or battery behaviour on low-end Android hardware. The Uber Lite figures cover the rider app only. naviG8r's driver population is on comparable or worse hardware and there is no published baseline to design against.

**Worth copying**

- Geofence-computed entitlement with platform-absorbed collection risk (Convoy): the app computes wait time from its own GPS, pays the driver on that evidence, and chases the customer separately. Removes the claim form entirely and removes the driver's reason to believe claiming is futile.
- Pre-filled claim from the app's own telemetry (Uber Freight): where a form is unavoidable, arrival and departure are filled from the app's GPS and the driver only confirms or corrects them. The driver is reviewing the platform's evidence, not producing his own.
- Reason-on-denial: every rejected claim renders the specific rule it failed, in the app, at the moment of rejection. Uber Freight states this explicitly for detention.
- Published, versioned rate card for every non-freight charge (detention per hour, cap, layover, cancellation, loading charge) on a public page the driver can screenshot and check the app's arithmetic against.
- Trust panel on the load card (DAT One): credit score, star reviews and average days-to-pay sit beside the rate and rate-per-mile, so the counterparty risk is priced on the same screen as the money.
- Rate locked at tap (Uber Freight, Truckstop): the number displayed at booking is the number paid, and any later change is a separate visible event with a reason.
- Auto-issued confirmation document at booking (Truckstop): the driver walks away holding a record, not a promise, generated the instant he accepts.
- Capture at the moment of completion with a legibility gate (Uber Freight): finishing the trip opens the camera, the driver confirms the photo is readable before it uploads, extra pages can be added, and deferral triggers repeated reminders. Applies equally to the new feedback-with-screenshots feature.
- Automation with a named human escape hatch (Loadsmart): unreadable, missing or ambiguous documents route to a person with the load context attached, rather than being force-parsed. Ship the confidence threshold and the queue in the same change as the model.
- Structured facility rating with fixed tags (Uber Freight: wait time, amenities, appointment flexibility, turning room, finding the entrance, staff politeness). Fixed tags beat free text for a tired driver, translate cleanly to another language, and quietly build a detention dataset.
- Visible scorecard with published thresholds, a stated recovery window and an in-app dispute control (Uber Freight Carrier Performance Program). If a score gates anything, the person it judges can see it, knows the number to hit, and can contest it.
- Event-based tracking milestones from an automated source rather than a screen held open (Uber Freight accepts app, web portal, ELD integration or an aggregator, and scores automation over the last 10 loads).
- Sort by what actually pays: deadhead, rate per mile, total price, weight — the four sorts Uber Freight exposes — rather than by recency or distance alone.

**Do not copy**

- A hard 24-hour claim deadline with no warning and no grace, enforced against a driver who spent those hours driving. Carriers report detention and lumper claims denied on the deadline alone, including a $260 receipt on a $760 load rejected as 'untraceable'.
- Gating free fast payment behind a performance tier whose largest input is tracking compliance. Uber Freight's free 2-day pay requires Top Carrier status, which requires 85%+ automated tracking updates — the carrier is effectively paid for surveillance rather than for service.
- Charging a percentage of the invoice to not be made to wait: 2.5% for 2-day payment against a 30-day standard, applied to accessorials too. Defensible only if the 30 days reflects a real customer-side constraint, stated as such.
- Requiring the app to be held open on screen for automatic location updates. It is the weakest of the accepted tracking methods, drains battery, and on an Indian budget Android with an aggressive OEM battery manager it will silently fail — then the driver is scored down for it.
- Scoring a carrier on actions outside their control or on a mis-tap: one reviewer notes 'Sometimes a teammate can click the wrong thing...that affects score'.
- Posting a take-it-or-leave-it rate while keeping the platform's margin invisible. FMCSA's 2024 proposal exists specifically because brokers contract carriers out of their statutory right to see the rate paid by the shipper.
- Canned support that promises a callback and does not make it, with no path to a human with authority — reported consistently across the App Store, Capterra and driver forums.
- Account suspension without warning or stated reason ('suspended my account without a warning and never replied my emails').
- Accessorials and surcharges surfacing after the headline quote — one shipper reports hidden charges raising the final cost by nearly 50% above the original quote.
- Treating an aggregate star rating as the feedback channel. The same product reads 4.8/5 on iOS and 3.6/5 on Capterra; neither tells the builder what broke.

**Gaps — looked for, not found**

- No primary, carrier-facing documentation from any of these apps describing how a multi-stop load is sequenced in the driver app, or whether proof of delivery is captured per stop. Uber Freight documents 'up to 10 stops' only on the shipper quoting side; everything else found was TMS vendor marketing. If multi-drop matters to naviG8r, this is original design work.
- No measured evidence that in-app document capture reduces payment disputes or shortens time-to-pay. Loadsmart's '70 to 90 percent' reduction in document-handling time is a vendor claim with no published method or sample.
- No published data from any platform on what share of detention or accessorial claims are approved versus denied, or on median time to resolution. Every claim about claim handling in this lens is either vendor policy text or an aggrieved user account.
- No independent (non-vendor) measurement of whether identity verification actually reduces fraud on load boards. Truckstop's 63,000-entity and 14,000-failed-check figures are self-reported in its own marketing report.
- No evidence of Hindi, or any non-English, interface support in any US digital freight app examined. These products are English-only in everything found, so this lens offers no pattern to copy for the multilingual goal — that needs a separate pass over Indian and other multilingual-market apps.
- overdriveonline.com and cnbc.com return HTTP 403 to automated fetch. The owner-operator survey figures on broker margins, and the verbatim Convoy shutdown memo, are therefore known to me only through search-result snippets, not the article text. I have not verified them against the source and they are excluded from the findings above.
- Could not verify in any primary Uber Freight document that facility ratings or estimated wait times are shown to a carrier before booking. Trade press (Transport Topics, Heavy Duty Trucking) reports it; the Uber Freight blog I read describes the ratings and the tag categories but does not say what appears pre-booking.
- DAT's marketing claim that brokers pay within 28 days on average with a mean credit score of 94/100 appeared only in a search snippet of dat.com; I did not fetch the page and have excluded the number from the findings.
- No India-specific comparison in this lens. Everything here is US freight; the regulatory frame (FMCSA, broker authority, ELD mandate) does not transfer, and the payment-terms norms almost certainly do not either. Treat the interaction patterns as transferable and the numbers as not.

**Worth copying**

- Multi-source trip tracking with automatic failover (FreightTiger): treat the phone's GPS as one source among several — phone GPS, FASTag toll crossings, carrier-supplied telematics — and fall back silently rather than showing a broken trip. FASTag pings alone give roughly 50 phone-independent position fixes per truck per month at zero hardware cost.
- Language before login: put the language picker on the first screen, ahead of OTP, and make it independent of the device language. Uber only offers in-app language selection on Android; drivers on hand-me-down or shop-configured phones cannot change the system language.
- Flat, mentally-computable fees instead of percentages (Namma Yatri): INR 25/day or INR 3.50/trip is a number a driver can check against his own arithmetic. A percentage of a freight rate he cannot audit is not.
- Three-step self-onboarding with a stated time budget (Porter): register, upload documents and attach vehicle, start receiving work — with training videos in the driver's own language. The market bar is under an hour, from home.
- Net-of-cost earnings, not gross freight: show toll and fuel against the trip on the same screen as the headline number. Tolls alone run INR 16,000-18,000 a month for a typical operator, so a gross figure is a misleading figure.
- Structured grievance categories drawn from what drivers actually escalate: payout dispute, toll reimbursement, account status, load did not match description, safety. The Porter union letter is a ready-made taxonomy. A free-text box will produce unusable feedback from a Class-5-literate user.
- Visible ticket state: number, status, and who is handling it. Every incumbent in this market is accused of never closing the loop, so closing it visibly is a cheap differentiator.
- Voice note as a first-class feedback input, with the audio kept as the record and the transcript treated as a hint. Hindi ASR is at roughly 22-32% WER on accented speech from this demographic, so transcribe for triage and never for adjudication.
- Photographic imagery over stylised icons for anything load-bearing, and never icons alone. Text-free interfaces beat text for low-literacy users, but abstract glyphs do not survive contact with them.
- Annotated screenshot capture on shake or on a persistent button, auto-attaching app version, OS, device model, carrier and last network state — so the engineer can reproduce without a conversation the driver cannot have in English.
- Appealable decisions with notice and evidence before any account block. This is the specific thing Ola, Uber and Porter scored zero on in Fairwork India 2024, and it is cheap to build before there are many accounts to block.
- An e-way bill validity countdown surfaced to the driver (1 day per 200 km, 20 km for over-dimensional cargo). It is a real legal deadline with a check-post consequence and no consumer app shows it.

**Do not copy**

- Grievance channels that route the complaint back into the system that caused it, with no human and no resolution state. The recurring driver complaint across Porter, BlackBuck and Vahak is 'I raised it and nothing happened'.
- Suspending or blocking an account without notice, evidence, or a right of reply — the specific practice the Telangana union escalated to a state labour minister over Porter in December 2025.
- Taking a subscription fee up front against a promise of loads that may not arrive. This is the dominant allegation in Vahak reviews and it poisons trust in the whole category, so naviG8r inherits the suspicion whether or not it does this.
- Branding on transparency while staying opaque about your own take. Namma Yatri published an open dashboard and drivers still say the company hides its earnings — a transparency claim raises the bar rather than clearing it.
- A zero-fee launch promise with no stated commitment about future fees. Drivers explicitly cite this fear: 'When they started, they said no fee. What if they increase this fee later?'
- Quoting a headline rate and settling toll, waiting time and cancellation separately and later. Porter drivers asked for waiting and cancellation charges to be automatic precisely because they were not.
- Language selection buried in a settings screen behind sign-in, or inherited from device language only — which locks out any driver who did not configure his own phone.
- Requiring the driver to keep a specific screen open for location sharing. On the Xiaomi/Oppo/Vivo/Realme skins that dominate this market it will fail silently anyway, and it shifts the blame for a platform limitation onto the driver.
- Icon-only navigation adopted as a literacy fix. The research says abstract glyphs are semantically opaque to exactly the users the simplification was meant to help.
- Ratings or scoring that affect load allocation without showing the driver the inputs or letting him contest them.
- Collecting more than the feature needs under a consent notice the driver cannot read — a DPDP problem as well as a trust problem, given the notice must be available in an Eighth Schedule language at the driver's option.
- Assuming a US-market default: a literate English-reading owner-operator with a late-model phone, continuous LTE, a bank account he manages himself, and a company that pays him by ACH. Every one of those is wrong here.

**Gaps — looked for, not found**

- No published, measured uninstall or churn rate for any Indian trucking or driver app. Nothing found on how many drivers abandon Porter, BlackBuck, Vahak or a carrier's own app, or when. The closest evidence is a six-study PRISMA review of truck-driver health apps that does not state any study was in India.
- No published breakdown of Indian long-haul truck drivers' home states or first languages. Searching produced only migration-crisis coverage and diaspora reporting, nothing occupational. The assumption that Hindi is the right first language is plausible but unsourced here — naviG8r should measure it from its own sign-up data before committing.
- No independent evaluation of Bhashini's ASR or translation quality on Indian road-freight vocabulary. Government self-description only. Any use in a feedback pipeline needs an internal eval against real driver audio first.
- Google Play listing details — supported language list, minimum Android version, install counts, rating distribution — for the Porter, BlackBuck, Vahak and Namma Yatri driver apps. Play Store pages could not be fetched. Only a mirror figure for Porter Driver (39 MB, 10M+ installs) was obtained, and mirrors are not authoritative.
- No evidence either way on whether an in-app feedback button with screenshot capture produces usable feedback from low-literacy users. All the bug-reporting tool material found is vendor marketing aimed at English-literate consumer apps, with no response-rate or quality numbers.
- A Hyderabad survey of 60 platform workers (45% account suspension, 66% ineffective grievance mechanisms, 56% unable to reach a representative) appeared in a search summary but could not be traced to a primary source. Deliberately excluded from the findings above rather than cited unverified.
- Why Rivigo's relay model failed. The shutdown is reported; no primary account of the cause was found in this pass.
- Current smartphone ownership among Indian truck drivers. The only figure found (7% in 2015 to 70% in 2019) comes from an unattributed teardown and should not be quoted as fact.
- Whether naviG8r's actual user mix matches the SaveLIFE 93%-employee / 6%-owner split. That study is from 2020 and surveyed drivers at transport hubs, not app users. This is a question for naviG8r's own data, not for the web.
- The cash-expense breakdown exhibit in the BlackBuck industry report (repairs 55-60%, driver wages 50-55%, fuel 25-30%, toll 5-10%) sums to well over 100% and its denominator is ambiguous. Excluded rather than guessed at.

**Worth copying**

- Transliterate-then-classify. Detect script; if the feedback is Latin-script Hindi, transliterate to Devanagari before the classifier sees it, not translate to English. Measured to recover most of the romanized-input gap (GPT-4o 75.3 to 80.1 F1, native baseline 81.3).
- Uncertainty-based selective routing. The Script Gap authors' own mitigation: classify everything cheaply, and route only low-confidence items to a stronger model or a human. Fits a feedback queue exactly, where volume is low and a wrong route is cheap to correct.
- Raw-first storage. Persist the driver's text exactly as typed, in its original script, in its own column. Detected script, transliteration, classification and any translation are derived columns that can be recomputed when the model changes. Never overwrite the original.
- Script-agnostic ingestion. Assume one free-text column will receive Devanagari, Latin-script Hindi, English and mixtures, because Gboard makes all of them one tap apart. Store a detected-script field rather than a language setting copied from the app locale.
- Voice note as the artifact, transcript as the derivative. Keep the audio file; show the transcript as editable and clearly provisional. At 26.8% WER on spontaneous phone speech, a transcript presented as fact is a lie with a number attached.
- Structured-first, free-text-last feedback. Screenshot plus one tap on a category plus an optional voice note, with typing as the last resort. Every field you can make tappable is a field that does not need language detection, transliteration or an LLM.
- Untranslated-messages-file as a CI gate. Flutter emits the list of messages missing from each locale for free; failing the build on a non-empty list is the cheapest defence against a second locale silently rotting back to English.
- Per-app language via the Android 13 LocaleManager API, paired with a readable-without-reading first-run choice. The system picker survives reinstall and matches what Uber's driver app does; the first-run screen covers the driver who will never open Settings.
- Devanagari layout pass. No letter-spacing on Hindi strings, generous line-height for matras above and below the baseline, and truncation by word rather than by character so conjuncts are not split. Test with the longest Hindi string, not the English one.
- Do not bundle a font on Android. AOSP ships Noto Sans Devanagari UI in the system fallback chain. Bundle one only for the web build, where CanvasKit is where Devanagari actually breaks.
- Leave numerals in Latin digits. CLDR already defaults hi to latn. Rupee amounts, weights and distances are the part a low-literacy driver reads most reliably; do not 'complete' the localisation by converting them.

**Do not copy**

- Auto-switching the app to Hindi from the device locale with no visible way back. A driver who borrowed a phone, or whose phone shipped with a locale they did not choose, is then locked out of an interface they could previously use.
- Presenting an ASR transcript as though it were what the driver said. At roughly one word in four wrong on spontaneous phone audio, echoing the transcript back as a confirmed record of a complaint is a fabricated record.
- Showing only the LLM's English translation of the driver's feedback in the ops dashboard. The person acting on it can no longer see what was actually written, and any mistranslation becomes invisible and permanent.
- Machine-translating the whole UI once at launch and never touching it again. Flutter's fallback is silent, so a stale Hindi locale degrades to a screen that is half Hindi and half English with no warning to anyone.
- Gating a language choice behind a text-only Settings menu, when the user who most needs the other language is the one least able to read the menu that contains it.
- Asking for extra data — phone contacts, a full name, a photo of the driver — as the price of leaving feedback. The feature needs the text, the screenshot and the trip it relates to. Nothing else.
- Auto-escalating or auto-penalising based on an LLM sentiment score on code-mixed text, where the measured benchmark performance is near chance on a two-way task.
- Converting numerals to Devanagari digits in the name of thorough localisation, removing the one element of the screen a low-literacy user could already read.
- Letting the feedback form insist on typing. For a driver who cannot type comfortably in either script, a mandatory text field is a closed door with a submit button on it.

**Gaps — looked for, not found**

- No empirical study of what a second locale costs to MAINTAIN in a mobile app — words per release, hours per sprint, defect rate from stale strings. Everything returned was localisation-vendor pricing pages quoting per-word rates, which is self-interested and does not measure ongoing effort. This is the gap I would most want closed before committing to Hindi as a supported locale rather than an experiment.
- No evidence at all on script preference among Indian truck drivers specifically. Every Roman-versus-Devanagari figure I found comes from social-media corpora (Twitter, urban users) or marketing surveys. Generalising urban Twitter behaviour to a 45-year-old owner-operator in a Hindi-belt district is a guess, not a finding. A short in-app poll would answer this better than more searching.
- Could not open the primary for the Nature Humanities and Social Sciences Communications paper on Hinglish code-mixing — nature.com returns a 303 to an auth gate. The 44.9%-to-56.3% figures are from a search-index summary of it and are unverified against the paper.
- Could not open the ACM TALLIP paper on deromanization of Hindi-English code-mixed text and its effect on toxic-comment classification and sentiment analysis (dl.acm.org returned HTTP 403). This is the paper that would independently replicate the transliterate-then-classify finding; right now that strategy rests on one study (Script Gap, arXiv 2512.10780) plus its own internal ablation. Single study, not a replicated finding.
- No measured evaluation of LLM classification of freight or logistics feedback in Hindi or Hinglish. The closest real-world evidence is the health-triage domain. Domain transfer is plausible but unverified — truck-domain vocabulary (lane, LR, challan, diesel advance, detention) is exactly the kind of term a general model and a transliterator both get wrong.
- No Flutter-specific measured data on Devanagari rendering defects on ANDROID. I found web/CanvasKit issues and a Noto font bug fixed in v2.002, but nothing measuring Android-target Flutter rendering correctness for Hindi. I did not run the app with Hindi text to check; that is an unverified area, not a clean one.
- No measured APK-size cost for bundling a Devanagari font, and no measurement of whether the AOSP fallback is present on the budget Android devices Indian drivers actually use. I verified the font is in AOSP's fonts.xml; I did not verify it is present on a specific low-end OEM build.
- Uber's actual list of driver-app languages in India. The official help page explains how to change the language but does not enumerate the options; the language lists I found come from news articles and marketing pages, not from Uber.
- Whether Vahak, Porter or BlackBuck's driver apps genuinely ship Hindi UI, and how they handle free-text input. All I found were third-party comparison blogs, which are the weakest evidence class here and often repeat each other.
- Smartphone penetration among Indian truck drivers is stale. The only figure I found (76% feature phone, 16% smartphone-with-internet) is from a 2017 survey and is almost certainly wrong by now in the direction of more smartphones. Do not plan against it.
- No evidence on whether an LLM should see the transliterated text, the original, or both. The Script Gap study replaced the input; nobody I found tested giving the model both the raw Latin text and its Devanagari transliteration together, which is the obvious thing to try and may be cheap to test in-house.

The backend is Prisma, so the feedback table is a schema addition plus one endpoint. | [api.flutter.dev](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/rendering/RenderRepaintBoundary/toImage.html) |

**Worth copying**

- Auto-attach the hard stuff, ask only for the human-only stuff. The user gets one required tap (category) and one optional free text or voice note; the app silently supplies app version, build, screen name, role, active load/trip id, connectivity, locale and the last N user actions. This is the direct design consequence of the Bettenburg mismatch result, and it is the difference between a feedback row you can act on and one you cannot.
- Persistent entry point, never a gesture. A "Help and feedback" item that is always in the same place, plus a contextual "Something wrong on this screen?" affordance on the trip and earnings screens. Shake, two-finger swipe and screenshot-detection triggers all fail in a moving truck and are undiscoverable to a first-time user.
- Screenshot pre-attached, visible, removable, blurrable. Capture at the instant the user taps feedback (before the form covers the screen), show it as a thumbnail they can open, offer blur and crop, and let them delete it in one tap. Rasterise any blur into the uploaded bytes and strip EXIF server-side - a drawn overlay is not redaction.
- Category chips before free text. Six to eight domain categories with icon and label, drawn from Maalej's four-type taxonomy adapted to naviG8r (payout, load or matching, trip and GPS, app broken, feature request, something else). The tap gives the triage model a strong prior and gives a low-literacy or hurried driver a way to report something without typing at all.
- Voice note as a first-class body, not an afterthought. Record in-app, upload the audio, transcribe server-side, store transcript and audio together, and show the transcript back for confirmation. Given ~16% WER in noisy Indian field conditions, the audio is the record and the transcript is the index.
- Reversible low-signal bucket. The triage model may file something as spam or low-signal, and that decision must be visible and undoable in your internal view. Nothing a driver sends is ever deleted by a model.
- Separate budgets for prompts and buttons. Proactive prompts ("How did that delivery go?") get a hard per-user annual cap in the style of Apple's three-per-365-days, fired only after a completed action such as a confirmed delivery, and never while a trip is in progress. The always-available feedback button has no cap at all.
- Store raw first, label second. Persist the verbatim text or audio, detected language and script, and the auto-attached context as immutable rows; write model labels (category, severity, language, duplicate cluster) into separate nullable columns. When the taxonomy changes - and it will once Hindi arrives - you can relabel without having lost anything.
- Triage as an offline job with an eval set, not an inline LLM call. A nightly or on-write classification pass over stored rows, with a held-out labelled set of your own feedback (including a Hinglish slice) and a tracked accuracy number. This is the only way the LLM triage claim survives an interview question.
- Close the loop visibly. Give the submission a reference number in the confirmation, and show the driver their past submissions with a status. Costs one screen; it is the difference between a feedback channel people use twice and one they use once.
- Cheap versus product, stated plainly. Cheap (days, in-house): Prisma table, one POST endpoint, screenshot via RepaintBoundary, category chips, free text, object storage for the image, an internal list view. A product in itself (do not build): session replay, automatic repro-step reconstruction, network log capture, crash symbolication, a deduplication engine, an annotation editor with layers. Those are why Instabug and Shake exist as companies.

**Do not copy**

- Shake-to-report as the invocation. Vendors default to it, and it is wrong for a phone mounted in a truck. Copy the floating button, not the gesture.
- Review gating - asking "Are you enjoying naviG8r?" and routing only the happy answers to the Play rating flow. Google's own documentation forbids asking any question before or while presenting the prompt, including that one.
- Putting a button on the Play in-app review flow. The quota is undisclosed and the app cannot detect whether the dialog appeared, so the button can do visibly nothing.
- Uploading a screenshot the user never saw. Every shipped SDK shows the capture before sending; silently attaching it is the version that turns into a privacy incident when a driver's screen had someone else's phone number on it.
- Attaching precise GPS to every feedback item because geolocator is already a dependency. It is a separate Play data-safety declaration and a separate DPDP notice line, and most feedback does not need it. Attach the trip id instead, and location only when the category is trip or GPS related.
- Making phone number or email mandatory to submit. Sentry makes everything except the message optional; requiring contact details to complain is a friction tax on exactly the users most likely to be unhappy.
- Asking the user for what the app already knows - app version, screen, load id, device model. Every such field is effort spent on information you could have attached for free, and it is the specific mistake the bug-report research indicts.
- Model-deleted feedback. Auto-spam classification without a recoverable bucket and a visible flag means you will never know what the model threw away.
- Machine-translating Hindi input and keeping only the English. The translation is derived data; the driver's own words are the record, and you will need them the first time a triage label is disputed.
- Prompting for feedback mid-trip. A proactive prompt while the driver is moving is a safety problem before it is a UX problem; the completed-delivery moment is the only safe proactive trigger in this app.
- Building the feedback channel with no reply path. A form that produces no acknowledgement, no reference number and no visible outcome trains drivers that reporting is pointless, and the submission rate will decay to near zero regardless of how good the form is.

**Gaps — looked for, not found**

- No peer-reviewed or primary measurement of how a passive in-app feedback button's placement or wording affects submission rate. Every response-rate number I could find (Refiner, Retently and similar) is a vendor aggregate about prompted surveys, which is a different mechanism. Treat any "good" submission rate target as unfounded until you have your own baseline.
- No published rate at which user-submitted feedback screenshots contain personal data. The redaction sources found are vendor blogs and SEO content, not studies. The mechanism (markup layers are not redaction, EXIF persists) is checkable; the prevalence is not.
- No public documentation or credible teardown of how Uber, Lyft or the Indian freight apps structure their in-app driver feedback flows. Their help-centre pages describe user-facing steps, not what is captured or how it is triaged. Anything claimed about their internals would be a guess.
- No measured comparison of voice-note feedback versus typed feedback - not on submission rate, not on downstream usefulness. The low-literacy evidence supports offering voice; it does not tell you how many drivers will use it or whether the resulting items are more actionable.
- No replicated evidence for LLM triage of product feedback. The strongest study found was single-site with 90 tickets and one human grader; the requirements-engineering evaluation reported F1 0.47-0.68. Both are single studies, and neither is on transport or Indic-language feedback.
- No Hindi-specific measurement of LLM classification of user feedback. The closest evidence is Marathi (LLMs trailing fine-tuned BERT by 10-14% accuracy) and general Indic benchmarks. Hindi is better resourced than Marathi, so the real gap is probably smaller - but that is reasoning, not a measurement.
- Did not verify Apple's TestFlight feedback flow (screenshot annotation, what is auto-attached). Apple's documentation pages did not render through WebFetch, and naviG8r is Android-only today, so I stopped rather than cite second-hand descriptions.
- Did not verify the Whisper Hindi WER figures against a primary benchmark paper. The 5.33% / 13.06% numbers come from a model card via search snippet and are read-speech in any case; the field-recording benchmark (16.2% Hindi WER) is the number I would actually plan against.
- Did not check whether any Indian carrier or driver app has published a DPDP-compliant multilingual consent notice that could be used as a reference implementation. The statutory requirement in Section 5(3) is verified; what good compliance looks like in practice is not.

## Synthesis — the ranked recommendation

Source: five research passes on rideshare driver apps, US digital freight, Indian road-freight apps, Indic multilingual/low-literacy design, and in-app feedback capture. No new research was run for this write-up; every claim below traces to a labelled finding in those passes.

### A framing correction before the ranking

The brief treats owner-operator and fleet driver as two equal personas. One measured source disagrees: SaveLIFE Foundation's national study (N=1,217 drivers, 101 fleet owners, 10 cities, Feb 2020) found more than 93% of truck drivers work as employees and about 6% drive a self-owned truck (https://savelifefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/design-single-page-27th-feb-2020.pdf). That study is six years old, surveyed drivers at transport hubs rather than app users, and predates the current smartphone wave. It is not a reason to deprioritise the owner-operator — he is the one who controls whether the truck uses your app — but splitting design effort 50/50 on the assumption of equal population is unsupported. **Check naviG8r's own sign-up mix before the next design cycle.** That is a query against your own database, not a research question.

Second framing point: the four things the owner-operator is actually buying are time-to-next-load (Indian trucks spend 24-48 hours finding the next shipment and idle 10-12 days a month, per the Redseer report filed with Zinka/BlackBuck's SEBI prospectus, https://bb-website-public-assets.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/legal-assets/pdf/Industry+Report.pdf), getting paid what was agreed, not losing money on costs he did not price, and not being blamed for the platform's failures. The five items below are ordered against those, not against feature novelty.

---

### The five things ranked by what they are worth to a driver

### 1. A trip record that cannot silently die

**What it is.** Replace "GPS shared while one screen is open" with: a foreground service, disk-persisted queues for accept / start trip / breadcrumb / confirm delivery that complete locally and replay when signal returns, dependent actions queued behind their parents, a loud named message whenever the server later rejects a queued action, and a server-side gap detector that tells the carrier a trip went dark rather than pretending it did not. Add FASTag toll crossings and any carrier telematics as secondary position sources.

**Why it is first.** Three independent lines of evidence converge:

- [observed] The Android OEM skins that dominate this market — Xiaomi/MIUI, Oppo/ColorOS, Vivo/FuntouchOS, Realme — run power managers that kill background work independently of stock Doze, `PowerManager.isIgnoringBatteryOptimizations()` can return true while the OEM manager still kills the process, and Xiaomi requires an explicit autostart permission that is off by default (https://dontkillmyapp.com/). The current design does not just have a UX limitation; on these handsets the location stream dies with no error and no one knows.
- [observed] NHAI told TRAI and DoT on 6 January 2026 that 424 locations covering roughly 1,750 km of the National Highway network have inadequate mobile coverage (https://www.medianama.com/2026/01/223-nhai-trai-improve-highway-mobile-coverage/). An online round-trip at "start trip" or "confirm delivery" is a design that strands drivers on documented dead stretches.
- [documented] Uber's Optimistic Mode is the worked answer: pending requests serialised to disk so they survive app restarts, dependent requests queued behind parents, server errors rolling state back, and a global Alert Framework built specifically because silent rollbacks left drivers in states they could not explain. Uber reports an average 13.5 seconds saved per optimistic operation as of November 2018 (https://www.uber.com/en-IN/blog/driver-app-optimistic-mode/).
- [documented] Uber Freight accepts three tracking sources — app, portal, ELD/telematics integration, aggregator — and treats the held-open app as the weakest (https://help.uber.com/en/freight/carrier/article/how-does-automated-tracking-impact-my-tracking-automation-score?nodeId=6da1335f-ddfe-405f-81a6-adcd9cfddfe8). FreightTiger's Indian answer is four simultaneous sources with automatic failover (https://www.freighttiger.com/transportation-management-system?product=ft-trace), though that is vendor self-description with no published accuracy numbers.

**What it is worth to a driver.** Everything downstream. A dead GPS trail is a wait-time claim he cannot make, a delivery he cannot prove, and — if you ever score reliability — a reputation hit for a platform defect. Today the blame for a silent OEM kill lands on him.

**Build cost.** The largest of the five. A durable local queue, server-side idempotency keys on every trip mutation, an ordering model for dependent actions, a foreground service with an OEM-specific autostart prompt during onboarding, and a gap detector on the server. Multi-week, and the kind of work that is much cheaper before there is a user base with live trips to migrate.

**What gets harder afterwards.** Every trip action becomes eventually consistent, so the server must stay idempotent forever and the client can lead the server. You now own a rollback-notification surface you did not have. Debugging moves from "read the server log" to "reconcile two logs." And you inherit a permanent support burden explaining battery permissions to drivers.

**Note on data cost.** Do not shrink payloads to save the driver money. TRAI puts average realisation at INR 7.51 per GB for Jan-Mar 2026 (https://aninews.in/news/business/indias-internet-base-crosses-1092-million-in-jan-mar-2026-broadband-grows-58-per-cent-qoq-trai20260622182406/). The binding constraints are coverage, battery and device memory. On memory: India's sub-$100 smartphone segment fell 59% year-on-year in Q1 2026 and memory prices rose roughly fourfold since September 2025 (https://counterpointresearch.com/en/insights/india-smartphone-market-q1-2026), so the device floor is getting worse, not better.

### 2. Proof at both ends, and a document in his hand at acceptance

**What it is.** Two small changes. At accept, mint an immutable trip confirmation record on the spot — Truckstop's Book It Now sends the rate confirmation "the moment your load is booked" (https://truckstop.com/product/load-board/book-it-now/), so the driver walks away holding a record rather than a promise. At delivery, gate "confirm delivery" on a photo plus a short numeric code read out by the consignee, both queued offline.

**Evidence.**
- [documented] Uber Eats makes the completion action unavailable until proof of delivery is captured (https://help.uber.com/en/merchants-and-restaurants/article/delivery-faqs?nodeId=39d229f3-4375-4cd8-848b-9df7d19102c1). The blocking prompt is the pattern.
- [documented] Uber Freight opens the camera on "Complete delivery" and asks the driver to confirm legibility before upload — "If all the signatures are legible tap the blue checkmark. If the image is unclear, retake the photo" (https://help.uber.com/en/freight/carrier/article/using-the-uber-freight-app-during-and-after-delivery?nodeId=37bbed18-dd0a-47aa-86eb-15b5f73bd0b8). The legibility check is the cheap win: a blurry POD found three days later by a back-office reviewer is a payment delay the driver cannot fix from 400 km away.
- [documented] Uber's Verify My Ride gives a 4-digit PIN the driver enters, five attempts allowed (https://help.uber.com/en/riders/article/whats-virtual-ride/?nodeId=2ddbb5e8-0dd3-4048-b9ee-f6b5e5311e25). A code issued at dispatch and validated against a hash stored locally works with no signal and no signature pad — which matters given the NHAI coverage gaps above.

**Worth to a driver.** This is the single largest category of freight payment dispute, and it is the one where his word currently loses to the consignee's. Caveat stated plainly: I have verified the mechanic at Uber and Uber Freight, not that it reduces disputes — see "what the research did not establish."

**Build cost.** Small, days rather than weeks, once item 1's offline queue exists. The photo is one screen; the code is a field at dispatch plus a hash comparison.

**What gets harder afterwards.** You now hold images, which means object storage costs, a retention policy, a Google Play Data safety declaration under "Photos" (https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10787469?hl=en), and an internal answer to "who at naviG8r can open a delivery photo." Disputes will now turn on your artifact, so its integrity becomes your problem. And the consignee code is an operations problem on the customer side, not an engineering one — someone at the drop point has to be told the number.

---

### 3. An offer card that shows net, not gross

**What it is.** Four additions to the load card: the empty-run distance to the loading point, an estimated toll and diesel cost for the lane with the assumptions stated, the resulting net per kilometre, and a counterparty panel — who the customer is, how many trips they have run on naviG8r, and how many days they actually took to pay last time. Plus a written commitment that the number shown at tap can rise if the load changes but can never fall.

**Evidence.**
- [documented] DAT One's load detail screen puts company name, MC#, reviews, an Ansonia credit score and "Days to Pay" beside the rate and rate-per-mile (https://one.support.dat.com/6-search-loads-4ef778b5/understanding-load-details-5a156463). For a man with one truck, "will this person pay" is a bigger risk than "is the rate good."
- [documented] Uber commits that where pickup and dropoff are unchanged the upfront fare can only increase, and the driver keeps the full amount if he finishes faster (https://help.uber.com/en/driving-and-delivering/article/upfront-fares?nodeId=bc83ed7e-6725-41de-afcb-72d263e5589f). This one-way ratchet is the cheapest trust commitment in the whole research set.
- [documented] Lyft added an estimated dollars-per-hour figure on the accept screen so drivers stop doing the arithmetic (https://help.lyft.com/hc/en-us/all/articles/8668928544).
- [documented] Tolls run INR 16,000-18,000 a month across roughly 50 FASTag swipes for a typical Indian operator, and FASTag reached 98% of toll collection by March 2024 (Redseer/Zinka prospectus, link above). A gross freight figure that ignores toll is a misleading figure, and FASTag is the one digital rail this user already touches 50 times a month and trusts.
- [documented] Uber's Long Pickup Premium pays standard rates past a city threshold, capped at $20 (https://help.uber.com/en/riders/article/long-pickup-premiums?nodeId=f3d14b75-7d9a-4938-a6d4-d0b2033ad13f). The empty run to the loading point is uncompensated in Indian road freight and is the main reason a good lane gets refused.

**Worth to a driver.** It changes which loads he takes. Every other item on this list improves a trip he has already accepted.

**Build cost.** Moderate, and mostly data rather than UI. Toll estimation per lane, a diesel price source and a stated mileage assumption, empty-run distance from his declared position, and a payment history that only exists after you have trips. The counterparty panel is genuinely blocked until you have volume — ship it as "first load with this customer" rather than faking a score.

**What gets harder afterwards.** Every number shown becomes a promise. Publish the assumptions — diesel price, date, assumed km/l — or the net figure becomes a grievance the first time it is wrong. The ratchet removes your ability to reprice downward after acceptance, permanently. And showing days-to-pay exposes your slowest customers to your drivers, which is a commercial conversation you should have before the feature ships, not after.

**Two hard prohibitions on this screen.** Never gate load information behind an acceptance rate — Uber does exactly this in Australia and New Zealand, requiring 85%+ acceptance before a driver may see trip duration and direction (https://www.uber.com/nz/en/blog/pro-anz-duration-direction). Information needed to judge a job is not a loyalty reward. And never let a timeout count as a refusal; Uber's acceptance rate treats an expired request identically to an active decline over a 100-request lookback (https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/understanding-acceptance-and-cancellation-rates/), which converts a coverage problem into a permanent reputation problem for a driver who was loading, eating or in one of NHAI's 424 dead spots.

---

### 4. Wait time paid automatically from the app's own GPS, with naviG8r carrying the collection risk

**What it is.** Compute dwell at pickup and drop from your own geofence, pay the driver on that evidence past a published threshold, and chase the customer separately.

**Evidence.**
- [observed] Convoy did exactly this: detention triggered past two hours at a facility, and critically "When drivers wait more than two hours for one stop, Convoy will initially charge the shipper. But if the shipper doesn't pay, Convoy will pay the carrier" (https://www.fleetowner.com/technology/article/21702193/convoy-rolls-out-automatic-detention-pay-feature).
- [measured] OOIDA's 2023 Detention Time Survey (253 responses from 18,788 members emailed, self-selected, ±6% at 95% confidence) found 12% never attempt to claim, of whom 44% said the reason is "the belief that they will not receive it anyway"; 17% collect no detention pay at all; and 50% said they lose one to two loads per week to detention (https://www.ooida.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2023-Detention-Time-Survey-FINAL.pdf). **The dominant barrier is learned futility, not ignorance of the rule.** A claim button will not fix that; automatic payment will.
- [measured] The US DOT Inspector General audit (ST2018019, 31 Jan 2018) estimated a 15-minute increase in average dwell raises the expected crash rate by 6.2%, and put the earnings cost at $1.1-1.3 billion a year for for-hire truckload drivers (https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/FMCSA%20Driver%20Detention%20Final%20Report.pdf). The same report's own finding is that accurate industrywide detention data do not exist.
- [documented] Where a form is unavoidable, Uber Freight pre-fills it from telemetry — "Your arrival and departure are automatically provided by the GPS data in the Uber Freight app" — and renders a reason on denial (https://www.uberfreight.com/en-US/blog/how-to-request-detention-on-uber-freight). Its rate card is published: detention $40/hour capped at 5 hours, with an eligibility window stated verbatim (https://help.uber.com/en/freight/carrier/article/what-is-uber-freight%E2%80%99s-accessorial-policy-for-carriers?nodeId=993f6166-3606-40af-bdbd-ab27905c0567).

**Worth to a driver.** The largest new rupee amount on this list. It is ranked fourth only because it is the least buildable by engineering alone.

**Build cost.** High, and mostly not code. Accurate facility coordinates for geofencing, dwell computation, a published and versioned rate card shipped *before* the claim flow so the driver can screenshot it and check your arithmetic, and a balance-sheet decision to pay before you collect.

**What gets harder afterwards.** You become a credit provider with a receivables book. You need fraud controls, because a driver who parks 200 metres from the gate accrues money. Customer contracts have to carry the charge or you are funding it from margin. And a published rate card is a commitment you cannot quietly change — Uber stopped publishing its global rideshare take rate with quarterly reports as of Q1 2025 and it reads as an admission regardless of the reason (NELP, July 2025, https://www.nelp.org/app/uploads/2025/07/Unpacking-Uber-Lyfts-Take-Rates-July-2025-Update.pdf).

**One anti-pattern to refuse explicitly.** Do not copy Uber Freight's 24-hour claim deadline. Carriers report claims denied on the deadline alone against a driver who spent those hours driving (https://www.thetruckersreport.com/truckingindustryforum/threads/uber-freight-is-going-down-becarefull.2505539/). If you compute the claim yourself, you need no deadline at all.

### 5. Money and account decisions he can audit and appeal

**What it is.** One settlement screen showing the customer's freight, naviG8r's commission, wait-time pay, deductions and the net, together. A published commission. And notice, evidence and a right of reply before any account block, with a visible resolution state.

**Evidence.**
- [observed] Colorado SB 24-075, effective 1 February 2025, requires TNCs to show on a *single screen* the total the rider paid before tip, the amount that went to the driver, and the tip; Uber sued to block it (https://towardsjustice.org/2024/06/07/press-release-rideshare-industry-transparency-bill-becomes-law/). "On a single screen" is the design requirement, not the disclosure.
- [documented] FMCSA's November 2024 NPRM on Transparency in Property Broker Transactions would void contract clauses making carriers waive the right to see the broker's records including the shipper-paid rate and the broker's margin (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/11/20/2024-27115/transparency-in-property-broker-transactions). The unresolved fight in US freight is whether the carrier sees the platform's cut. You can settle it by design instead of by regulation.
- [measured] FairFare crowdsourced 76,000+ trips to reconstruct the platform take rate precisely because platforms do not disclose it, and organisers report it shaped Colorado SB 24-075 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11273). An undisclosed cut gets reconstructed by outsiders, less flatteringly than the truth.
- [measured] Fairwork India Ratings 2024 scored 11 platforms out of 10; Ola, Uber and Porter all scored 0, and the low scorers lacked documented mechanisms for worker expression (https://inc42.com/buzz/ola-uber-porter-score-zero-points-on-fairworks-rating-for-gig-workers/). There is a published external rubric you can design against rather than retrofit.
- [observed] In December 2025 the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union wrote to the state labour minister about Porter citing accounts suspended without inquiry, no grievance redressal, delayed toll resolution, and driver fear of retaliation (https://www.medianama.com/2025/12/223-porter-gig-worker-exploitation-tgpwu/). That letter is a ready-made requirements list.
- [observed] Namma Yatri charges a flat INR 25/day or INR 3.50/trip instead of a percentage (https://restofworld.org/2025/uber-ola-copy-india-zero-commission-ride-hailing-app/). A number a driver can check against his own arithmetic will be trusted; a percentage of a freight rate he cannot audit will not. The same piece records drivers still distrusting the company — a transparency claim raises the bar rather than clearing it.

**Worth to a driver.** Lower in immediate rupees than items 1-4, higher than any of them in whether he keeps the app installed. Drivers multi-home by default; Namma Yatri drivers run several apps at once.

**Build cost.** Low to moderate. One screen over a ledger model, plus a decision log with reason codes and a reply path. The blocker is not the UI — it is that the ledger must reconcile with your payment rail, and the payout architecture question (does naviG8r need an RBI Payment Aggregator licence or a partner, given non-bank aggregators must hold funds in escrow with a single scheduled commercial bank and settle T+1, https://www.fidcindia.org.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/RBI-PAYMENT-AGGREGATORS-DIRECTIONS-15-09-25.pdf) should be settled before the payouts feature grows.

**What gets harder afterwards.** Publishing the commission means you cannot vary it silently, ever. The appeal path needs a human on a rota, which is an ops cost that scales with users. And a fee for fast settlement becomes hard to defend: Uber charges $1.75 per Instant Pay and Lyft $1.75 per Express Pay to reach money already earned (https://help.lyft.com/hc/en-us/all/articles/115012923167-Express-Pay), and on UPI/IMPS rails that would be a toll, not cost recovery.

### Build order differs from value order

Value order is 1-5 above. Dependency order is 1, then 2 and 5 in parallel, then 3, then 4. Item 1 is the precondition for 2 and 4. Item 5 is cheap and its cost rises with every account you create before it exists. Item 4 needs a commercial decision that should start now even though the build comes last.

### One thing explicitly not on this list

A learned pricing or matching model. Convoy's co-founder said the pricing automation took about five years to reach 98-99% of loads at better-than-industry margins, called it "an expensive thing to teach a system how to get pricing right," and the company still shut down (https://www.freightwaves.com/news/convoy-co-founder-reflects-on-digital-brokerages-rise-and-fall). The asset that survived was the carrier-facing matching and verification layer: Flexport bought it for roughly $16 million and sold it to DAT for about $250 million within roughly 18 months, concluding it "needed to be a neutral infrastructure layer" (https://www.flexport.com/blog/why-we-bought-built-and-sold-the-convoy-platform/). Pricing intelligence is a roadmap item, not a phase.

### Which findings above are weak evidence

Stated plainly, because several of the most quotable numbers in this set are the least reliable.

**Vendor self-description, no independent measurement:** Uber Freight's pricing and accessorial policy pages; Uber Lite's India device statistics (Uber measuring Uber's own users); FreightTiger's multi-source failover claim, which publishes no accuracy numbers; Loadsmart's "70 to 90 percent" document-handling reduction, which has no published method or sample; Truckstop's 63,000-entity and 14,000-failed-identity-check fraud figures, self-reported in its own marketing report; Porter's "under an hour" onboarding claim; Bhashini's quality claims, which are government self-description with no independent evaluation on freight vocabulary.

**Commissioned or conflicted:** The Redseer industry report is filed with Zinka Logistics' IPO prospectus and is self-interested about the size of the opportunity — its description of the user is unusually blunt and useful, its market sizing is an argument. The Everee survey of 419 gig drivers (70% prefer daily or instant pay, 65% took payday loans over payout delays) was published by a vendor selling instant-pay infrastructure; the direction matches what is known about Indian owner-operator cash flow, but the numbers should not be quoted. The NELP take-rate fact sheet is advocacy — treat its framing as argument and its underlying citations as the evidence.

**Single study, not replicated:** the UCR working paper on algorithmic wage discrimination is headed "Preliminary: Comments welcome. Please do not cite or circulate," so the mechanism it describes is a risk to design against and the coefficient is not a fact; the DOT IG detention audit is one federal audit whose own title says industrywide detention data do not exist; SaveLIFE's literacy and employment split is one 2020 study at transport hubs; the PRISMA review on truck-driver app engagement rests on six studies of health apps, none stated to be in India; the Script Gap transliterate-then-classify result rests on one paper plus its own internal ablation, and the paper that would replicate it (ACM TALLIP on deromanization) returned HTTP 403 and was not read; the Lahaja Hindi ASR benchmark, the IndicWhisper numbers and the agricultural field-recording benchmark are each single benchmarks.

**Self-selected or tiny samples:** OOIDA's detention survey is 253 responses from a trade association, skewed toward the aggrieved; More Perfect Union's pay-discrepancy claim is seven drivers run by a campaigning organisation and is disputed by Uber; Capterra's Uber Freight rating is n=29 against 13,000 on iOS; MouthShut reviews of Vahak, Porter and BlackBuck are self-selected negatives — useful for *what* people complain about, useless for base rates.

**Out of domain or out of date:** Bettenburg's bug-reporting result (the one that most strongly justifies auto-attaching context) is 2008 open-source reporting by technical reporters — the direction transfers, the percentages do not; Medhi's low-literacy banking study is 2011 and about banking; the KPMG-Google Indian-language finding is a 2017 industry survey; Refiner's in-app survey response rates are a vendor aggregate whose headline reversed between its own 2022 and 2025 reports, and it measures prompted surveys, not a passive button.

**Unattributed and excluded:** the 7%-to-70% driver smartphone figures come from a teardown with no named source and should not appear in any deck; the Hinglish 44.9%-to-56.3% Twitter figures come from a search-index summary because nature.com returns an auth gate.

### What the research did not establish

- **That pre-acceptance transparency improves anything measurable.** No controlled or quasi-experimental evidence that showing more information before acceptance improves driver earnings, retention or satisfaction. Every source argues from complaint volume, take-rate accounting or advocacy. The case for items 3 and 5 is strong on ethics and regulation and unmeasured on outcomes. Say that out loud rather than implying a business case that does not exist.
- **That in-app document capture reduces payment disputes or shortens time-to-pay.** No measurement found anywhere. Item 2's justification is mechanical, not empirical.
- **Claim approval rates or resolution times for detention at any platform.** Every claim about claim handling in the research is vendor policy text or an aggrieved user account.
- **Churn or uninstall rates for any Indian trucking or driver app.** Nothing published on when or why drivers abandon Porter, BlackBuck or Vahak.
- **An India-specific ranking of what drivers complain about.** The only systematic complaint research is US and UK — a CSCW study of 1.39 million Reddit comments (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10768) and an Oxford UK pay study. The ranking may not transfer. Check it against naviG8r's own Slack feedback before treating it as requirements.
- **A driver-side pattern for multi-stop loads.** Uber Freight documents "up to 10 stops" only on the shipper quoting side; nothing found on how stops are sequenced in a driver app or whether POD is captured per stop. If multi-drop matters in Indian road freight, that is original design work with its own discovery.
- **Whether an in-app feedback button with screenshots produces usable feedback from low-literacy users.** All the bug-reporting material found is vendor marketing aimed at English-literate consumer apps, with no response-rate or quality numbers. There is also no measured comparison of voice-note versus typed feedback on either submission rate or downstream usefulness.
- **Script preference among Indian truck drivers.** Every Roman-versus-Devanagari figure comes from urban social-media corpora. Generalising Twitter behaviour to a 45-year-old operator in a Hindi-belt district is a guess.
- **LLM triage quality on freight or logistics feedback in Hindi or Hinglish.** The closest measured evidence is health triage and Marathi classification. Domain terms — LR, challan, diesel advance, detention, halt — are exactly what a general model and a transliterator both get wrong.
- **What a second locale costs to maintain.** No empirical study of words per release, hours per sprint or defect rate from stale strings. This is the gap most worth closing before committing to Hindi as a supported locale.
- **Crash, ANR and battery baselines on the device class naviG8r's drivers carry.** Uber Lite's figures cover the rider app. There is no published baseline to design item 1 against; you will have to create your own.
- **Whether the AOSP Devanagari fallback is present on specific budget OEM builds.** The font is in AOSP's fonts.xml (https://github.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/blob/master/data/fonts/fonts.xml); that it ships on a given Realme or Vivo build was not verified.
- **Why Rivigo's relay model shut down.** The shutdown is reported; no primary account of the cause was found. Do not cite a reason.

### Hindi: a roadmap item with a cheap first step

**What full localisation would take.** The driver app has no localisation infrastructure at all — no `intl` or `flutter_localizations` dependency, no `l10n.yaml`, no ARB directory, no bundled fonts, `generate: true` not set (verified by reading `/Users/rishabhnatarajan/AIProjects/naviG8r/apps/driver_pilot/pubspec.yaml`; `lib/` was not grepped to count hardcoded strings). So Hindi is a from-scratch build: a string extraction pass over `lib/`, ARB scaffolding and gen-l10n, translation of freight vocabulary that no general translator handles well, and a Devanagari layout pass — no letter-spacing on Hindi strings, truncation by word not character so conjuncts are not split, and more line-height for matras above and below the baseline (W3C Devanagari Gap Analysis, https://www.w3.org/TR/deva-gap/). Two things are free: Android ships Noto Sans Devanagari in the system fallback, so no font needs bundling for the Android target, and CLDR's default numbering for the `hi` locale is `latn`, so rupee amounts and distances stay in Western digits (https://unicode.org/reports/tr35/tr35-numbers.html). Leave that alone — numerals are the part of the screen a low-literacy driver reads most reliably. The web build is the risk: the Devanagari rendering failures found are CanvasKit issues (https://github.com/flutter/flutter/issues/84028), so passing on an Android emulator tells you nothing about the web target.

**What it would buy, honestly.** Less than it appears. SaveLIFE found 8.8% of truck drivers illiterate and 37.1% educated only to Class 5 — roughly 46% at or below Class 5. Translating the UI reaches the driver who reads Hindi and does nothing for the driver who reads nothing; icons, numerals and voice carry that user. And swapping words for glyphs is not the fix either: research on low-literacy interfaces finds abstract icons are frequently semantically opaque to exactly those users, who prefer concrete photographic representations (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/21582440231172741). Where Hindi does earn its place is trust on money screens: around 70% of Indian-language internet users in the KPMG-Google work considered local-language content more reliable than English (2017 industry survey, unreplicated, https://kpmg.com/ky/en/home/insights_new/2017/04/indian-language-internet-users.html). Language choice affects whether he believes the earnings number, not only whether he can read it.

**The one part that is not optional.** DPDP Act 2023 section 5(3) requires the notice to be available "in English or any language specified in the Eighth Schedule," which includes Hindi (https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section5.html). The moment the feedback flow captures a screenshot, logs or location, it needs a notice, and that notice must be available in Hindi on request. Continuous GPS already triggers this. So Hindi arrives as a compliance obligation on one bounded surface long before it arrives as a product decision.

**The cheap first step, in order, none of which commits to full localisation:**

1. **Ship the consent notice for feedback and location in Hindi and English.** Two paragraphs of prose, statutory, bounded, and required anyway.
2. **Capture `locale` and a detected-script field on every feedback row from day one.** That is how you learn whether Hindi demand is real — from your own drivers, not from a 2017 survey. It costs one column.
3. **Do the string extraction pass now, regardless of whether Hindi ships.** It is the part that is painful to retrofit and it is what makes the feedback feature's own strings translatable later.
4. **Put a language choice on the first screen, ahead of OTP, visible without reading**, wired to the Android 13 per-app language API — even if it currently offers only English. Uber's driver app changes language through the per-app setting (https://help.uber.com/en/driving-and-delivering/article/setting-preferred-language?nodeId=7e5211b5-05f7-49cd-8093-3cc2fe7c1681), which survives reinstall but is undiscoverable for the user who most needs it. Many Indian drivers use a hand-me-down or shop-configured phone whose system language they did not choose. The placement is the expensive decision; the strings are not.
5. **Run a one-question in-app poll on script preference.** This answers a question the web cannot.
6. **Wire `untranslated-messages-file` into CI before the first Hindi string lands** (https://docs.flutter.dev/ui/internationalization). Flutter's locale fallback is silent — a missing Hindi string renders in English with no warning to anyone — so a build that fails on a non-empty list is what stops the second locale rotting.

**What not to do.** Do not auto-switch from device locale with no visible way back. Do not machine-translate the UI once at launch. Do not convert numerals to Devanagari in the name of thoroughness. And do not extrapolate a successful Hindi pilot into Marathi, Kannada or Telugu without re-measuring: in the Script Gap tables Hindi's romanized penalty was about 3 F1 points while Kannada fell 83.7 to 65.4 and Marathi 78.6 to 61.4 (https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10780v1). Hindi is the mildest case and its results will flatter you.

### In-app feedback: the minimum version worth building

Separate feature, separate decision, buildable now. The architecture check is real: the driver app currently depends only on `dio`, `flutter_secure_storage`, `go_router`, `google_maps_flutter`, `geolocator` and `razorpay_flutter`, and capturing your own widget tree via `RenderRepaintBoundary.toImage` needs no new dependency and no Android permission, unlike MediaProjection screen capture (https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/rendering/RenderRepaintBoundary/toImage.html). The backend is Prisma, so this is a table, an endpoint, object storage and one internal list view.

**The design principle, and it has the best evidence in the whole research set.** Bettenburg et al. surveyed 466 respondents across Apache, Eclipse and Mozilla: developers rated steps to reproduce most helpful (83%) and screenshots mid-value (26%); reporters rated steps to reproduce among the hardest to provide (51% difficulty) and screenshots the easiest (8%). Correlation between developer-rated importance and reporter provision was -0.035, and incomplete information was the most severe problem at 74% (https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/bettenburg-fse-2008.pdf). **The valuable items are the ones users find hardest to supply.** So the app supplies them and spends the driver's one unit of effort on what only he knows. Caveat: 2008, technical open-source reporters, not Hindi-speaking truck drivers. The direction transfers; the percentages do not.

**Minimum version:**

- **Entry point: a persistent "Help and feedback" item in a fixed place, plus a contextual affordance on the trip and earnings screens.** Never shake. Instabug defaults to shake with a sensitivity threshold of 650 (https://docs.luciq.ai/docs/android-invocation) and a phone in a dash mount on an Indian highway shakes constantly. Never two-finger swipe either — gloves.
- **One required input: a category tap.** Icon plus label, drawn from what Indian drivers actually escalate (the Telangana union letter) crossed with Maalej's validated four-type taxonomy, which reached 88-92% precision and 90-99% recall with a supervised classifier (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00766-016-0251-9). Categories: payment not received or wrong amount; toll or expense reimbursement; load did not match description; trip or GPS problem; app is broken; account or sign-in; feature request; something else.
- **Optional body: voice note or free text, typing never mandatory.** Medhi et al. found that in a mobile banking task not one low-literacy subject completed a transaction with a text interface, while 72% completed with spoken dialog and 100% with a text-free graphical UI (https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/1959022.1959024; 2011, banking, direction transferable and percentages not). **Keep the audio as the record and treat the transcript as an index** — the best Hindi WER on a 10,934-recording benchmark of noisy rural field audio was 16.2% (https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03868), which is roughly one word in six. Showing a transcript back as the driver's verbatim words would be fabricating a record.
- **Screenshot: captured at the tap, shown, removable, blurrable.** Capture before the form covers the screen, present it as a thumbnail, offer blur and crop, allow one-tap delete. Rasterise the blur into the uploaded bytes — a drawn overlay leaves the pixels recoverable — and strip EXIF server-side. Sentry treats crop-before-send as a privacy control rather than a nicety and makes every field except the message optional (https://docs.sentry.io/platforms/javascript/user-feedback/). Never upload an image the driver did not see; a driver's screen can carry a consignee's phone number.
- **Auto-attach silently:** app version, build, screen name, role (owner-operator or fleet driver), active load or trip id, connectivity state, whether GPS sharing was on, locale, detected script, and the last N user actions. Not precise GPS by default — `geolocator` being already present is not a reason; location is a separate Play declaration and a separate DPDP notice line. Attach the trip id, and location only when the category is trip or GPS related.
- **Storage: raw first, labels second.** Persist the verbatim text or audio and the auto-attached context as immutable columns; write any model labels into separate nullable columns. You will change the taxonomy when Hindi arrives, and relabelling must not cost you the original.
- **Close the loop visibly.** A reference number in the confirmation and a list of past submissions with a status. This is the differentiator, not the form. The recurring allegation across Porter, BlackBuck and Vahak is identical — raised it, nothing happened (https://www.mouthshut.com/product-reviews/vahak-reviews-926105280). A channel with no acknowledgement and no visible outcome trains drivers that reporting is pointless and the submission rate decays regardless of form quality.
- **Consent and compliance in the same change:** a plain-language notice at the point of capture, available in Hindi, plus Play Data safety declarations for Photos and Other user-generated content.
- **Keep the Play rating flow strictly separate.** Google's In-App Review API forbids asking the user any question before or while presenting the prompt, including "do you like the app", and warns against a button trigger because the undisclosed quota means the dialog may not appear (https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-review). Review gating is ruled out by Google's own documentation, which saves the argument.
- **Proactive prompts get a hard annual cap; the button gets none.** Apple's three-per-365-days is a sane borrowed limit (https://developer.apple.com/app-store/ratings-and-reviews/). The only safe proactive trigger in this app is confirmed delivery. Never prompt mid-trip — that is a safety problem before it is a UX problem.

**Explicitly not in v1:** LLM triage, session replay, automatic repro-step reconstruction, network log capture, a deduplication engine, an annotation editor with layers. The last five are why Instabug and Shake exist as companies.

**On LLM triage specifically, and this is the eval discipline the feature lives or dies by.** Build it as an offline job over stored rows, not an inline call, and do not ship auto-routing until you have measured it on 100-200 rows of your own labelled feedback including a Hinglish slice. The published numbers do not justify trust by default: a 2025 evaluation of five lightweight open-source LLMs on user-feedback classification reported F1 of roughly 0.47-0.68 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23055), clearly worse than Maalej's 2016 supervised baseline; the strongest positive result found was 89% correct categorisation with kappa 0.85 on 90 support tickets graded by a single human reviewer (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13242945/). And English quality does not carry: on three Marathi tasks GPT-4o and Llama 3.1 405B trailed a fine-tuned BERT baseline by 10.2% and 14.1% accuracy (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17637). When Hindi feedback arrives, detect script and transliterate Latin-script Hindi to Devanagari before classification rather than translating to English — measured to recover most of the romanized gap, GPT-4o F1 rising 75.3 to 80.1 against a native-script baseline of 81.3 (https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10780v1) — but note that rests on one study whose replication I could not open, and that it deliberately converts cheap Latin text into Devanagari, which costs 4.08x English tokens on cl100k_base and 1.34x on o200k_base (https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24276). Record the tokenizer alongside the model when you price it. Never auto-escalate or auto-penalise on a model sentiment score: on GLUECoS English-Hindi, mBERT reaches 95.87 F1 on language identification but only 58.24 F1 on sentiment (https://arxiv.org/html/2004.12376v2) — routing is reliable, judgement is not. Any low-signal bucket must be visible and reversible; nothing a driver sends is ever deleted by a model.

**The success metric is issues reproduced and fixed, not submissions counted and not an average score.** The same product reads 4.8/5 on iOS and 3.6/5 on Capterra (https://www.capterra.com/p/206000/Uber-Freight/reviews/); neither number tells a builder what broke. And there is no published baseline for what a good passive-button submission rate looks like, so treat your first month's number as the baseline rather than measuring it against anything found on the web.
